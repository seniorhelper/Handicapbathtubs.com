Handicap tubs in Rhode Island: get half back, up to $4,500

How the state's Livable Home grant works, and what Medicaid and the VA add.

Call 1-888-779-2284Get a free quote

By Zach Wennstedt, Aging Safely Baths. Updated November 17, 2026. Program rules checked November 17, 2026.

Direct answer: Rhode Island's Livable Home Modification Grant reimburses 50% of an accessible bathroom project, up to $4,500, for a resident with a disability whose own income is under $78,700 (no dependents). Walk-in tubs and walk-in showers qualify. Approval must come before work starts. Medicaid plans and the VA can help too, but none of them pays twice for the same cost.

Who runs Rhode Island's bathroom grant, and who qualifies?

The grant belongs to the Governor's Commission on Disabilities (GCD), which administers it under state regulation 525-RICR-20-00-3. It is paid entirely from state money, was created by the legislature in 2018, and works as a reimbursement: you pay the contractor, then send proof and get up to half back.

The test has three parts, and it is more generous than many people expect:

  • A disability, not an age. The applicant must have an impairment that substantially limits a major life activity. The GCD's examples include limited use of an arm or leg, vision or hearing loss and memory loss. A person in their 40s after a spinal cord injury qualifies on the same terms as someone in their 80s with arthritis.
  • Your own income, not the household's. The limit uses the applicant's prior-year adjusted gross income, so a spouse's paycheck does not count against you.
  • Homeowners and renters. Renters can apply. The property owner signs an affidavit that the accessible features will stay for ten years. Properties owned by an LLC, an S corporation, a cooperative housing corporation or another corporation are excluded.

Does the grant pay for a walk-in tub or a roll-in shower?

Yes. The GCD's FAQ lists a walk-in shower or walk-in tub among qualifying bathroom work, along with grab bars and a raised toilet and sink, and AARP Rhode Island's 2026 budget testimony describes the program as helping pay for walk-in and roll-in showers. The regulation itself uses the broader phrase "an accessible bathroom," and the project must include at least one feature that meets an existing accessibility standard. A therapist's note tying the tub or shower to the disability is what turns a remodel into an eligible modification.

What the grant will not touch: removable equipment that could be carried to another home, anything health insurance would reimburse, and costs another local, state or federal program is already paying. The same modification cannot receive two grants.

How busy is the program, and is money running short?

Demand has climbed every year. In a December 2025 presentation to the legislature's commission on home repairs, the GCD reported the number of grants paid and the average check by fiscal year. The average payment rose from $2,579 in FY2022 to $3,301 in FY2025, and early FY2026 grants averaged $3,572.

Source: GCD and OSCIL presentation to the Commission on Whole Home Repairs, December 15, 2025. Bar height is the average payment; the cap line sits at $4,500.

The same presentation carried a warning. The program's budget had run around $700,000 to $800,000 a year for five years, the last budget was cut by $250,000, and the GCD said applicants would have to go on a waiting list in FY2027. In March 2026, AARP Rhode Island asked the House Finance Committee to restore funding to the FY2026 level. If you are planning a bathroom for next year, apply early in the state fiscal year, which starts July 1.

What order do the steps go in?

Most grant problems come from doing things in the wrong order. The regulation is specific:

  1. Get a needs assessment from a qualified healthcare professional who does not work for your contractor: a physician, physical or occupational therapist, or a certified aging-in-place specialist.
  2. Get an itemized quote. Work outside the eligible categories, such as a new vanity or tile upgrade, has to be listed separately. The contractor also signs an affidavit that the required building permits will be pulled before work begins.
  3. Apply with proof of income, such as last year's W-2s or federal return. The GCD says applicants hear back within about a week on whether the file is approved, denied or incomplete.
  4. Wait for the award letter before any work starts. The only exceptions cover people discharged from a hospital or facility, or unable to get home or to appointments after a diagnosis.
  5. Start within 120 days. The award expires unless the GCD receives a claim, notice that work has begun or a signed contract within 120 days of mailing.
  6. Claim after the work is done: before and after photos, the scope of work, paid invoices, proof of payment, permits, and a healthcare professional's sign-off that the work meets the standard. Claims for projects finished before June 30 are due by July 10.

If costs go up mid-project, tell the GCD. Without an amended award letter, the reimbursement cannot exceed the original amount.

Will Rhode Island Medicaid pay for a roll-in shower or walk-in tub?

Usually not for the remodel itself, but it can pay for the equipment around it. Neighborhood Health Plan of Rhode Island's environmental and home modification policy, updated August 19, 2026, is a useful example of how a Medicaid health plan draws the line. It lists roll-in showers and tub cuts as excluded, along with structural remodeling, moving plumbing fixtures and standard fixtures such as tubs. It can cover tub slider systems, rolling shower chairs, grab bars and ceiling or wall-mounted lifts when a licensed therapist or certified assistive technology professional recommends them and the owner or landlord signs a property agreement.

That pairing is often the practical answer: the Livable Home grant helps with the shower or tub, and the health plan covers the shower chair or the lift. Ask your plan's care manager what your own policy says, because coverage differs by plan and program.

For people who have run out of other options, the Ocean State Center for Independent Living (OSCIL) runs home access programs as a funder of last resort. Its listed bathroom work includes roll-in shower conversions, tub cuts, grab bars and tub slider systems, for Rhode Island residents with a disability who meet its income limit and have less than $25,000 in cashable assets.

What can Rhode Island veterans add?

The VA's HISA benefit is separate from the state grant and can apply to the same bathroom: a lifetime maximum of $6,800 when the need ties to a service-connected condition or the veteran is rated 50% or higher, and $2,000 for others. It covers essential bathroom work such as roll-in showers, but not spa or jetted features. In Rhode Island the request starts with a VA clinician at the Providence VA Medical Center, 830 Chalkstone Avenue, or one of its clinics.

Two cautions when stacking. The state grant will not reimburse any cost HISA is already paying, so the quote should show which line items each program covers. And both programs want approval before work begins, so nothing gets scheduled until both letters are in hand. Veterans with severe service-connected disabilities may qualify for the larger SAH and SHA housing grants; our VA SAH and SHA bathroom guide has the FY2027 amounts and the bathroom size rules.

Which accessible bath fits the person?

The grant pays the same whichever you choose, so choose by the transfer. A roll-in shower suits someone who stays in a shower wheelchair or needs a helper. A walk-in tub suits someone who walks in, sits steadily and wants to soak. A wheelchair transfer tub with an outward door suits someone who slides across from a chair. Our condition-by-condition tub guide goes deeper, and Aging Safely Baths, which installs throughout the state, covers housing, permits and delivery in its Rhode Island walk-in tub and roll-in shower page.

Rhode Island has a lot of people this matters to. AARP Rhode Island told lawmakers in 2026 that more than 31% of residents are 55 or older, against 28% nationally, and that adults with disabilities are 13% of the state's population.

Rhode Island questions

How much is the Rhode Island Livable Home Modification Grant?

It reimburses 50% of the eligible cost, up to $4,500 per project. The cap rose from $4,000 to $4,500 in a 2023 amendment to the regulation. Any project over $9,000 receives the full $4,500 if approved.

Can a renter in Rhode Island get the grant for a roll-in shower?

Yes, renters can apply. The property owner must sign an affidavit that the accessible features will remain for ten years, and rental properties owned by an LLC or corporation are excluded.

Can I start the bathroom work before I am approved?

No, in most cases. Work may not begin until the Governor's Commission on Disabilities issues an award letter. Exceptions cover people discharged from a health care facility or unable to return home after a diagnosis.

Does Rhode Island Medicaid pay for a walk-in tub?

Generally not. Neighborhood Health Plan of Rhode Island's 2026 policy excludes roll-in showers, tub cuts and standard fixtures, though it can cover tub slider systems, rolling shower chairs and lifts with a therapist's recommendation. Ask your own plan.

Can I use the state grant and VA HISA together?

Yes, on different costs. The state grant cannot reimburse anything another government program is paying, so the itemized quote should show which program covers which lines, and both approvals should arrive before work starts.
