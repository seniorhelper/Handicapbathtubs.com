How to plan a wheelchair accessible bathtub layout

By Zach Wennstedt, Aging Safely Baths. First published 2026; updated October 15, 2026 with ADA measurements.

Direct answer: Plan the layout around the transfer, not the tub. The wheelchair needs a clear strip beside the tub (the ADA uses 30 inches wide along its full length), the door must open fully without hitting the toilet or vanity, the door should hinge on the side that suits the bather's stronger side, and there should be a straight path in and out of the room.

When you choose a wheelchair accessible bathtub, it is easy to focus on jets, heated seats and finishes. The layout decides whether any of that gets used. A good layout makes every transfer predictable. A poor one turns a quality tub into an obstacle course.

1. Trace the path from the doorway

Start in the hallway and follow the route the wheelchair will take.

  • Can the chair turn into the bathroom doorway, and is the opening wide enough? The ADA uses a 32-inch minimum clear width for doors in public buildings, a useful target at home.
  • Where can the chair park beside the tub seat? That spot should be reachable going forward, without three-point turns on a wet floor.
  • Is there room to turn around, or will the user back out? The ADA's turning space is a 60-inch circle or a T-shape within a 60-inch square.

Sketch the room on paper with the chair's length and width. The free walk-in tub planner has a page for measurements.

2. Choose the door side and swing

Transfer tubs swing outward and come in left-hand and right-hand versions. Ask:

  • Which direction does the bather transfer most safely, toward a stronger arm or leg?
  • When the door is fully open, does it hit the toilet, vanity or wall?
  • Where does a caregiver stand, and does the open door trap them?

There is no universal best side. In one bathroom a left-hinged door lets the chair park parallel to the seat; in another a right-hinged door keeps knees clear of the toilet.

3. Check the plumbing side, but do not let it decide

If the existing drain is on the left and the new tub's drain is on the right, the installer may need to move lines or open the floor. That adds cost, but it is usually a smaller problem than a door on the wrong side for the bather. Get both options priced.

Inside the tub, check that the faucet and hand shower controls can be reached from the seat without twisting. The ADA puts tub controls on an end wall, between the rim and the grab bar, toward the open side.

4. Leave room for the wheelchair and the helper

The ADA asks for a clear strip at least 30 inches wide running the full length of an accessible tub, plus 12 inches past the head end when there is a permanent seat (section 607.2). At home that strip does two jobs: it is where the wheelchair parks and where a caregiver stands. If a helper is involved, see our caregiver bathing guide for how much space they need to bend at the knees and move their feet.

5. Match the layout to the transfer style

Transfer style and what the layout needs
Transfer styleLayout needs
Seated slide (with or without a board)Chair parks parallel and close to the seat; seat and cushion at similar heights; grab bar at the door edge
Stand-pivot with a helperNon-slip floor in front of the tub; room for the helper beside the chair; bar positioned for pushing up
Mechanical liftSpace for the lift base or a ceiling track over the tub; often a roll-in shower instead
Supported step-in (part-time chair user)Low threshold, strong bars and a place to park the chair on bad days

Step-by-step instructions for each method are in the transfer tub guide.

6. Keep privacy and dignity in the plan

If the only workable position leaves the bather in view of the hallway whenever the door is open, they may avoid using the tub. Check that the bathroom door can close with the wheelchair inside, and think about a curtain that does not interfere with the transfer.

7. Common layout mistakes

  • Door blocked: the tub door hits a vanity or toilet and cannot open fully.
  • Wet parking spot: floor slope sends water toward where the chair parks.
  • Nothing within reach: no shelf or niche reachable from the seat.
  • No blocking: walls with no reinforcement, so grab bars cannot be added later.
  • Designing for today only: no room for a transfer board or lift if needs change.

Questions people ask us

How much room does a wheelchair need to turn in a bathroom?

The 2010 ADA Standards use a 60-inch-diameter circle, or a T-shaped space inside a 60-inch square with arms and base at least 36 inches wide (section 304.3). Many home bathrooms are smaller, so plan a straight path in and out if a full turn will not fit.

Should the transfer tub door be on the left or the right?

Choose the side that lets the bather transfer toward their stronger side and lets the wheelchair park parallel to the seat without hitting the toilet or vanity. A therapist can confirm the side. Do not let existing plumbing alone decide it.

Can a transfer tub go in a standard 60-inch tub alcove?

Often, yes. Several transfer tubs are made in 52 to 60 inch lengths, and extension panels can close a gap. The bigger question is the floor space in front: the outward door needs room to swing and the wheelchair needs room to park beside it.

Sources
