Paying for a handicap bathtub with a disability: VA, Medicaid and vocational rehab

By Zach Wennstedt, Aging Safely Baths. First published October 9, 2026; rewritten and rechecked against official program pages October 15, 2026.

Direct answer: The main public sources are VA grants for veterans (HISA up to $6,800 or $2,000; SAH up to $126,526 and SHA up to $25,350 in fiscal 2026), Medicaid home and community-based services waivers in many states, and state vocational rehabilitation when the change supports a job. Original Medicare generally does not pay. Get a therapist's or doctor's written recommendation and program approval before work starts.

Veterans: three VA programs that can fund a bathroom

HISA: Home Improvements and Structural Alterations

HISA pays for medically necessary changes to a veteran's home, and the VA's own list of examples includes the use of essential lavatory and sanitary facilities, with roll-in showers named. The lifetime limit is:

  • Up to $6,800 for a service-connected disability, a disability treated as if service-connected, or any disability when the veteran has a single service-connected rating of at least 50 percent.
  • Up to $2,000 for other disabilities.

The application must include a prescription written or approved by a VA physician, with the diagnosis and medical justification. In practice, that usually starts with the veteran's VA primary care team and a therapy consult. Aging Safely Baths has a longer walkthrough of the HISA grant for bathroom modifications and a HISA bathroom planning guide with estimate paperwork.

SAH and SHA: adaptive housing grants

These are larger grants for veterans with specific, severe service-connected disabilities. For fiscal year 2026 the VA lists:

  • Specially Adapted Housing (SAH): up to $126,526. Qualifying conditions include the loss, or loss of use, of more than one limb; loss of use of a lower leg along with the lasting effects of a disease or injury; blindness in both eyes with 20/200 acuity or less; certain severe burns; and, for a limited number of people each year, loss or loss of use of one lower extremity after September 11, 2001.
  • Special Home Adaptation (SHA): up to $25,350. Qualifying conditions include loss or loss of use of both hands, certain severe burns and certain respiratory injuries.

The grant can be used up to six times over a lifetime. A veteran living temporarily with a family member may qualify for a Temporary Residence Adaptation grant of up to $50,961 (SAH-eligible) or $9,100 (SHA-eligible) for FY 2026.

An SAH-funded home must include an adapted bathroom with a roll-in shower at least 48 by 48 inches. Our SAH and SHA bathroom requirements guide lists every minimum from the VA design handbook and has a plan checker.

VR&E Independent Living

Veterans with a service-connected disability who cannot return to work right away, and who need services to live as independently as possible, may qualify for the Independent Living track of Veteran Readiness and Employment. The VA says services can include evaluation for a home adaptation grant as part of the rehabilitation plan, and generally last up to 24 months.

Medicaid home and community-based services waivers

Under section 1915(c) of the Social Security Act, states run waivers that pay for services at home for people who would otherwise need institutional care. Each state chooses who its waivers serve (for example older adults, people with physical disabilities, or people with intellectual disabilities), caps enrollment, and decides which services to include.

Many waivers include a service usually called environmental accessibility adaptations or environmental modifications. Two official examples:

  • Montana's waiver manual describes physical adaptations that let a member function with greater independence at home, and lists modification of bathroom facilities among the examples. It also requires the change to be functionally necessary and the most cost-effective way to meet the need. (Montana DPHHS)
  • New York's children's waiver guidelines list roll-in showers or accessible tubs among allowable environmental modifications, tied to an assessed need in the child's plan of care. (New York State Department of Health)

To find out what your state offers, ask your Medicaid case manager or the state Medicaid agency which waiver you are enrolled in, or could apply for, and whether it covers home modifications. Expect a home assessment, a cost cap and sometimes a waiting list. Our main site answers the narrower question does Medicaid pay for walk-in tubs.

State vocational rehabilitation

Every state has a vocational rehabilitation (VR) agency funded under the Rehabilitation Act. Federal rules list rehabilitation technology among the services VR can provide (34 CFR 361.48(b)(17)), and several state agencies have written policies on home modifications. The common thread is that the change must be needed to reach the employment outcome in your Individualized Plan for Employment. For example, being able to bathe and dress without a caregiver may be what lets someone get to work on time. A general wish for an easier bath will not qualify.

The U.S. Department of Education's Rehabilitation Services Administration keeps a directory of state VR agencies. Raise the bathroom when your counselor writes or updates your plan.

Medicare and private insurance

Original Medicare covers durable medical equipment that a doctor prescribes for use at home, but it generally does not pay for home modifications such as a new tub, or for items it treats as comfort or convenience, such as grab bars and bath seats. Some Medicare Advantage plans offer limited extra benefits for home safety items, so call the number on the member card and ask specifically. AARP has a plain summary of what Medicare covers for home safety.

Local help and taxes

  • Centers for Independent Living are run by and for people with disabilities of any age and often know local accessibility funding. Find one in the ILRU directory.
  • Area Agencies on Aging serve people 60 and older and can point to home repair programs. The Eldercare Locator (1-800-677-1116) connects you to yours.
  • USDA Section 504 Home Repair offers loans, and grants to homeowners 62 and older, for very-low-income owners in eligible rural areas. Check your address and income with your local USDA Rural Development office.
  • Tax deduction: IRS Publication 502 says improvements made to accommodate a home to a disabled condition, including modifications to bathrooms and grab bars anywhere, usually do not increase home value and can be included in full as medical expenses. You must itemize, and only medical expenses above 7.5 percent of adjusted gross income count. See IRS Publication 502 and ask a tax professional.

Apply in the right order

  1. Get the need in writing. A doctor's or occupational therapist's recommendation is the backbone of almost every application. Our transfer tub guide explains what an OT assessment covers.
  2. Ask about pre-approval. Most programs will not pay for work that is already finished.
  3. Collect written quotes. Programs often want an itemized estimate, sometimes two or three. We provide written quotes and product specification sheets on request.
  4. Check contractor rules. Some programs require licensed or approved contractors, permits or a final inspection.
  5. Stack carefully. A veteran may combine VA programs with a tax deduction; Medicaid and VR usually pay only for what other sources do not. Ask each program how it treats other funding.

If public programs do not cover everything, Aging Safely Baths offers financing options, and its grants and programs page lists more sources.

Questions people ask us

How much is the VA HISA grant for a bathroom?

HISA is a lifetime benefit of up to $6,800 for a service-connected disability, or for any disability if the veteran has a service-connected rating of at least 50 percent, and up to $2,000 for other eligible veterans. It needs a prescription written or approved by a VA physician.

What are the SAH and SHA grant amounts for 2026?

For fiscal year 2026 the VA lists up to $126,526 for a Specially Adapted Housing grant and up to $25,350 for a Special Home Adaptation grant. Eligibility depends on specific service-connected disabilities, and the money can be used up to six times.

Will Medicaid pay for an accessible tub?

Sometimes, through a home and community-based services waiver. Many state waivers include environmental accessibility adaptations, such as modifying a bathroom, when the change is needed for the person to keep living at home. Rules, caps and waiting lists vary by state.

Can vocational rehabilitation pay for a bathroom modification?

In some cases. Federal rules let state VR agencies provide rehabilitation technology as part of a plan for employment, and some states have written home modification policies. The modification must be tied to your work goal, not general home access.

Does Medicare pay for a walk-in or transfer tub?

Generally no. Original Medicare treats home modifications and most bathroom safety items as non-medical. Some Medicare Advantage plans offer limited extra benefits, so call your plan and ask.
