Ways to Pay for a Handicap Bathtub: VA, Medicaid and Local Help

Funding • Approx. 6 minute read • Published October 9, 2026

A handicap bathtub or roll-in shower is a big purchase, and most families pay for it out of pocket. But there are programs that can help, depending on who you are, where you live and why the change is needed. This guide walks through the most common options and the questions to ask before you apply.

Rules and funding change, and every program decides eligibility for itself, so treat this as a starting map, not a promise of coverage. Always confirm the details with the program directly.

1. Medicare: usually not, with one exception to check

Original Medicare generally does not pay for walk-in tubs, handicap bathtubs or bathroom remodels, because they are treated as home modifications rather than durable medical equipment. Some Medicare Advantage plans offer extra benefits for bathroom safety items. Coverage differs from plan to plan, so call the number on the back of the member card and ask specifically about home safety or bathroom modifications.

2. Veterans: the VA HISA grant and adaptive housing grants

The VA's Home Improvements and Structural Alterations (HISA) grant helps eligible veterans pay for medically necessary changes to the home, such as making a bathroom accessible. It is a lifetime benefit of up to $6,800 for veterans with a service-connected condition and up to $2,000 for other eligible veterans. A VA doctor's prescription is part of the process.

Veterans with more severe service-connected disabilities may qualify for the Specially Adapted Housing (SAH) or Special Home Adaptation (SHA) grants, which are larger. The VA updates their maximum amounts each fiscal year.

3. Medicaid waiver programs

Many states run Medicaid home and community-based services (HCBS) waivers that can pay for home modifications, sometimes including an accessible shower or tub, when the change helps someone keep living at home instead of moving to a facility. Each state sets its own rules, limits and waiting lists. A case manager or your state Medicaid office can tell you whether a bathroom modification is covered.

4. Local aging and disability agencies

  • Area Agencies on Aging connect older adults with local home repair and modification programs. The Eldercare Locator (1-800-677-1116) can point you to yours.
  • Centers for Independent Living serve people with disabilities of any age and often know local funding sources for accessibility work.
  • Nonprofit home repair groups in many areas help older or disabled homeowners with safety repairs at low or no cost.

5. Rural homeowners: USDA home repair help

The USDA's Single Family Housing Repair program (often called Section 504) offers low-interest loans to very-low-income homeowners in eligible rural areas, and grants to those 62 and older, to remove health and safety hazards. Your local USDA Rural Development office can confirm whether your address and income qualify.

6. Taxes: medical expense rules

IRS Publication 502 explains that some home improvements made mainly for medical care can count as medical expenses, generally for the part of the cost that does not increase the value of the home. Medical expenses are only deductible if you itemize and they pass the IRS threshold. Ask a tax professional before counting on a deduction, and keep the doctor's recommendation and your invoices.

7. Questions to ask before you apply

  • Does the program require approval before work starts? Many will not reimburse a project that is already finished.
  • Does it need a doctor's or therapist's recommendation?
  • Does it require specific contractors, multiple bids or a home assessment?
  • Is there a waiting list, and how long is it?
